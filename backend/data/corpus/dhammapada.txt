Chapter 1. Pairs

1:1 Intention is the leader of things; intention is first, they're made by intention. If with corrupt intent you speak or act, suffering follows you, like a wheel, the ox's foot.
1:2 Intention is the leader of things; intention is first, they're made by intention. If with pure intent you speak or act, happiness follows you like a shadow that never leaves.
1:3 "They abused me, they hit me! They beat me, they robbed me!" For those who bear such a grudge, hatred is never laid to rest.
1:4 "They abused me, they hit me! They beat me, they robbed me!" For those who bear no such grudge, hatred is laid to rest.
1:5 For never is hatred laid to rest by hate, it's laid to rest by love: this is an ancient teaching.
1:6 When others do not understand, let us, who do understand this, restrain ourselves in this regard; for that is how conflicts are laid to rest.
1:7 Those who contemplate the beautiful, their faculties unrestrained, immoderate in eating, lazy, lacking energy: Māra strikes them down like the wind, a feeble tree.
1:8 Those who contemplate the ugly, their faculties well-restrained, eating in moderation, faithful and energetic: Māra cannot strike them down, like the wind, a rocky mountain.
1:9 One who, not free of stains themselves, would wear the robe stained in ocher, bereft of self-control and truth: they are not worthy of the ocher robe.
1:10 One who's purged all their stains, steady in ethics, possessed of self-control and of truth, they are truly worthy of the ocher robe.
1:11 Thinking the inessential is essential, seeing the essential as inessential; they don't realize the essential, for wrong thoughts are their habitat.
1:12 Having known the essential as essential, and the inessential as inessential; they realize the essential, for right thoughts are their habitat.
1:13 Just as rain seeps into a poorly roofed house, lust seeps into an undeveloped mind.
1:14 Just as rain doesn't seep into a well roofed house, lust doesn't seep into a well developed mind.
1:15 Here they grieve, hereafter they grieve, an evildoer grieves in both places. They grieve and fret, seeing their own corrupt deeds.
1:16 Here they rejoice, hereafter they rejoice, one who does good rejoices in both places. They rejoice and celebrate, seeing their own pure deeds.
1:17 Here they're tormented, hereafter they're tormented, an evildoer is tormented in both places. They're tormented thinking of bad things they've done; when gone to a bad place, they're tormented all the more.
1:18 Here they delight, hereafter they delight, one who does good delights in both places. They delight thinking of good things they've done; when gone to a good place, they delight all the more.
1:19 Much though they may recite scripture, if a negligent person does not apply them, then, like a cowherd who counts the cattle of others, they miss out on the blessings of the ascetic life.
1:20 Little though they may recite scripture, if they live in line with the teaching, having given up greed, hate, and delusion, with deep understanding and heart well freed, not grasping to this life or the next, they share in the blessings of the ascetic life.

Chapter 2. Diligence

2:21 Heedfulness is the state free of death; heedlessness is the state of death. The heedful do not die, while the heedless are like the dead.
2:22 Understanding this distinction when it comes to heedfulness, the astute rejoice in heedfulness, happy in the noble ones' domain.
2:23 They who regularly meditate, always staunchly vigorous; the attentive realize extinguishment, the supreme sanctuary from the yoke.
2:24 For the hard-working and mindful, pure of deed and attentive, restrained, living righteously, and diligent, their reputation only grows.
2:25 By hard work and diligence, by restraint and by self-control, a smart person would build an island that the floods cannot overflow.
2:26 Fools and simpletons devote themselves to negligence. But the wise protect diligence as their best treasure.
2:27 Don't devote yourself to negligence, or delight in erotic intimacy. For if you're diligent and meditate, you'll attain abundant happiness.
2:28 When the astute dispel negligence by means of diligence, ascending the palace of wisdom, sorrowless, they behold this generation of sorrow, as an attentive one on a mountain top beholds the fools below.
2:29 Heedful among the heedless, wide awake while others sleep— a true sage leaves them behind, like a swift horse passing a feeble.
2:30 Maghavā became chief of the gods by means of diligence. People praise diligence, while negligence is always deplored.
2:31 A mendicant who loves diligence, seeing fear in negligence— advances like fire, burning up fetters big and small.
2:32 A mendicant who loves diligence, seeing fear in negligence— such a one can't decline, and has drawn near to extinguishment.

Chapter 3. The Mind

3:33 The mind quivers and quakes, hard to guard, hard to curb. The discerning straighten it out, like a fletcher an arrow.
3:34 Like a fish pulled from the sea and cast upon the shore, this mind flounders about, trying to throw off Māra's dominion.
3:35 Hard to hold back, flighty, alighting where it will; it's good to tame the mind; a tamed mind leads to bliss.
3:36 So hard to see, so subtle, alighting where it will; the discerning protect the mind, a guarded mind leads to bliss.
3:37 The mind travels far, wandering alone; incorporeal, it lies hidden in the heart. Those who will restrain the mind are freed from Māra's bonds.
3:38 Those of unsteady mind, who don't understand the true teaching, and whose confidence wavers, do not perfect their wisdom.
3:39 One whose mind is not festering, whose heart is undamaged, who's given up right and wrong, alert, has nothing to fear.
3:40 Knowing this body breaks like a pot, and fortifying the mind like a citadel, attack Māra with the sword of wisdom, guard your conquest, and never settle.
3:41 All too soon this body will lie upon the earth, bereft of consciousness, tossed aside like a worthless log.
3:42 A wrongly directed mind would do you more harm than a hater to the hated, or an enemy to their foe.
3:43 A rightly directed mind would do you more good than your mother or father or any other relative.

Chapter 4. Flowers

4:44 Who bestirs this earth, and the Yama realm with its gods? Who sets out the well-taught word of truth, as an expert a flower?
4:45 A trainee bestirs this earth, and the Yama realm with its gods. A trainee sets out the well-taught word of truth, as an expert a flower.
4:46 Knowing this body's like foam, realizing it's all just a mirage, and cutting off Māra's blossoming, vanish from the King of Death.
4:47 As a mighty flood sweeps off a sleeping village, death steals away a man even as he gathers flowers, his mind caught up in them.
4:48 The terminator gains control of the man who has not had his fill of pleasures, even as he gathers flowers, his mind caught up in them.
4:49 A bee takes the nectar and moves on, doing no damage to the flower's beauty and fragrance; and that's how a sage should walk in the village.
4:50 Don't find fault with others, with what they've done or left undone. You should only watch yourself, what you've done or left undone.
4:51 Just like a glorious flower that's colorful but lacks fragrance; eloquent speech is fruitless for one who does not act on it.
4:52 Just like a glorious flower that's both colorful and fragrant, eloquent speech is fruitful for one who acts on it.
4:53 Just as one would create many garlands from a heap of flowers, when a person has come to be born, they should do many skillful things.
4:54 The fragrance of flowers doesn't spread upwind, nor sandalwood, pinwheel, or jasmine; but the fragrance of the good spreads upwind; a true person's virtue spreads in every direction.
4:55 Among all the fragrances— sandalwood or pinwheel or lotus or jasmine— the fragrance of virtue is supreme.
4:56 Faint is the fragrance of sandal or pinwheel; but the fragrance of the virtuous floats to the highest gods.
4:57 For those accomplished in ethics, meditating diligently, freed through the highest knowledge, Māra cannot find their path.
4:58 From a heap of trash discarded on the highway, a lotus might blossom, fragrant and delightful.
4:59 So too, among those thought of as trash, a disciple of the perfect Buddha outshines with their wisdom the blind ordinary folk.

Chapter 5. The Fool

5:60 Long is the night for the wakeful; long is the league for the weary; long transmigrate the fools who don't understand the true teaching.
5:61 If while wandering you find no partner equal or better than yourself, then firmly resolve to wander alone— there's no fellowship with fools.
5:62 "Sons are mine, wealth is mine"— thus the fool frets. For even your self is not your own, let alone your sons or wealth.
5:63 The fool who thinks they're a fool is wise at least to that extent. But the true fool is said to be one who imagines that they are wise.
5:64 Though a fool attends to the wise even for the rest of their life, they still don't understand the teaching, like a spoon the taste of the soup.
5:65 If a clever person attends to the wise even just for an hour or so, they swiftly understand the teaching, like a tongue the taste of the soup.
5:66 Fools and simpletons behave like their own worst enemies, doing wicked deeds that ripen as bitter fruit.
5:67 It's not good to do a deed that plagues you later on, for which you weep and wail, as its effect stays with you.
5:68 It is good to do a deed that doesn't plague you later on, that gladdens and cheers, as its effect stays with you.
5:69 The fool imagines that evil is sweet, so long as it has not yet ripened. But as soon as that evil ripens, they fall into suffering.
5:70 Month after month a fool may eat food from a grass-blade's tip; but they'll never be worth a sixteenth part of one who has appraised the teaching.
5:71 For a wicked deed that has been done does not curdle quickly like milk. Smoldering, it follows the fool, like a fire smothered over with ash.
5:72 Whatever fame a fool may get, it only gives rise to harm. Whatever good features they have it ruins, and blows their head into bits.
5:73 They'd seek the esteem that they lack, and status among the mendicants; authority over monasteries, and honor among other families.
5:74 "Let both layfolk and renunciants think the work was done by me alone. In anything at all that's to be done, let them fall under my sway alone." So thinks the fool, their greed and pride only growing.
5:75 For the means to profit and the path to quenching are two quite different things. A mendicant disciple of the Buddha, understanding what this really means, would never delight in honors, but rather would foster seclusion.

Chapter 6. The Astute

6:76 Regard one who sees your faults as a guide to a hidden treasure. Stay close to one so intelligent and astute who curbs you when you need it. Sticking close to such an impartial person, things get better, not worse.
6:77 Advise and instruct; curb wickedness: for you shall be loved by the good, and disliked by the bad.
6:78 Don't mix with bad friends, nor with the worst of men. Mix with spiritual friends, and with the best of men.
6:79 Through joy in the teaching you sleep at ease, with clear and confident heart. An astute person always delights in the teaching proclaimed by the Noble One.
6:80 While irrigators guide water, fletchers bend arrows straight, and carpenters bend timber straight, the astute tame themselves.
6:81 As the wind cannot stir a solid mass of rock, so too blame and praise do not affect the wise.
6:82 Like a deep lake, clear and unclouded, so clear are the astute when they hear the teachings.
6:83 True persons give up everything, they don't cajole for the things they desire. Though touched by sadness or happiness, the astute appear neither depressed nor elated.
6:84 Never wish for success by unjust means, for your own sake or that of another, desiring children, wealth, or nation; rather, be virtuous, wise, and just.
6:85 Few are those among humans who cross to the far shore. The rest just run around on the near shore.
6:86 When the teaching is well explained, the people who practice accordingly will cross over Death's dominion so hard to pass.
6:87 Rid of dark qualities, an astute person would develop the bright. Having left home for homelessness in seclusion, where joy is hard,
6:88 they'd long for satisfaction there. Forsaking sensual pleasures, owning nothing, an astute person would cleanse themselves of mental corruptions.
6:89 And those whose minds are rightly developed in the awakening factors; who, letting go of attachments, delight in not grasping: with defilements ended, brilliant, they are quenched in this world.

Chapter 7. The Perfected Ones

7:90 At journey's end, rid of sorrow; everywhere free, all ties given up, no fever is found in them.
7:91 The mindful apply themselves; they delight in no abode. Like a swan gone from the marsh, they leave home after home behind.
7:92 Those with nothing stored up, who have understood their food, whose domain is the liberation of the signless and the empty: their path is hard to trace, like birds in the sky.
7:93 One whose defilements have ended; who's not attached to food; whose domain is the liberation of the signless and the empty: their track is hard to trace, like birds in the sky.
7:94 Whose faculties have become serene, like horses tamed by a charioteer, who has abandoned conceit and defilements; the unaffected one is envied by even the gods.
7:95 Undisturbed like the earth, true to their vows, steady as Indra's pillar, like a lake clear of mud; such a one does not transmigrate.
7:96 Their mind is peaceful, peaceful are their speech and deeds. Such a one is at peace, rightly freed through enlightenment.
7:97 Lacking faith, a house-breaker, one who acknowledges nothing, purged of hope, they've wasted their chance: that is indeed the supreme person!
7:98 Whether in village or wilderness, in a valley or the uplands, wherever the perfected ones live is a delightful place.
7:99 Delightful are the wildernesses where no people delight. Those free of greed will delight there, not those who seek sensual pleasures.

Chapter 8. The Thousands

8:100 Better than a thousand meaningless sayings is a single meaningful saying, hearing which brings you peace.
8:101 Better than a thousand meaningless verses is a single meaningful verse, hearing which brings you peace.
8:102 Better than reciting a hundred meaningless verses is a single saying of Dhamma, hearing which brings you peace.
8:103 The supreme conqueror is not he who conquers a million men in battle, but he who conquers a single man: himself.
8:104 It is surely better to conquer oneself than all those other folk. When a person has tamed themselves, always living restrained,
8:105 no god nor centaur, nor Māra nor divinity, can undo the victory of such a personage.
8:106 Rather than a thousandfold sacrifice, every month for a full century, it's better to honor for a single hour one who has developed themselves. That offering is better than the hundred year sacrifice.
8:107 Rather than serve the sacred flame in the forest for a hundred years, it's better to honor for a single hour a personage who has developed themselves. That offering is better than the hundred year sacrifice.
8:108 Whatever sacrifice or offering in the world a seeker of merit may make for a year, none of it is worth a quarter of bowing to the sincere.
8:109 For one in the habit of bowing, always honoring the elders, four blessings grow: lifespan, beauty, happiness, and strength.
8:110 Better to live a single day ethical and absorbed in meditation than to live a hundred years unethical and lacking immersion.
8:111 Better to live a single day wise and absorbed in meditation than to live a hundred years witless and lacking immersion.
8:112 Better to live a single day energetic and strong, than to live a hundred years lazy and lacking energy.
8:113 Better to live a single day seeing rise and fall than to live a hundred years blind to rise and fall.
8:114 Better to live a single day seeing the state free of death than to live a hundred years blind to the state free of death.
8:115 Better to live a single day seeing the supreme teaching than to live a hundred years blind to the supreme teaching.

Chapter 9. Wickedness

9:116 Rush to do good, shield your mind from evil; for when you're slow to do good, your thoughts delight in wickedness.
9:117 If you do something bad, don't do it again and again, don't set your heart on it, for piling up evil is suffering.
9:118 If you do something good, do it again and again, set your heart on it, for piling up goodness is joyful.
9:119 Even the wicked see good things, so long as their wickedness has not ripened. But as soon as that wickedness ripens, then the wicked see wicked things.
9:120 Even the good see wicked things, so long as their goodness has not ripened. But as soon as that goodness ripens, then the good see good things.
9:121 Think not lightly of evil, that it won't come back to you. The pot is filled with water falling drop by drop; the fool is filled with wickedness piled up bit by bit.
9:122 Think not lightly of goodness, that it won't come back to you. The pot is filled with water falling drop by drop; the attentive one is filled with goodness piled up bit by bit.
9:123 Avoid wickedness, as a merchant with rich cargo and small escort would avoid a dangerous road, or one who loves life would avoid drinking poison.
9:124 You can carry poison in your hand if it has no wound, for poison does not infect without a wound; nothing bad happens unless you do bad.
9:125 Whoever wrongs a man who has done no wrong, a pure man who has not a blemish, the evil backfires on the fool, like fine dust thrown upwind.
9:126 Some are born in a womb; evildoers go to hell; the virtuous go to heaven; the undefiled are fully extinguished.
9:127 Not in midair, nor mid-ocean, nor hiding in a mountain cleft; you'll find no place on the planet to escape your wicked deeds.
9:128 Not in midair, nor mid-ocean, nor hiding in a mountain cleft; you'll find no place on the planet where you won't be vanquished by death.

Chapter 10. The Rod

10:129 All tremble at the rod, all fear death. Treating others like oneself, neither kill nor incite to kill.
10:130 All tremble at the rod, all love life. Treating others like oneself, neither kill nor incite to kill.
10:131 Creatures love happiness, so if you harm them with a stick in search of your own happiness, after death you won't find happiness.
10:132 Creatures love happiness, so if you don't harm them with a stick in search of your own happiness, after death you will find happiness.
10:133 Don't speak harshly, they may speak harshly back. For aggressive speech is painful, and the rod may spring back on you.
10:134 If you still yourself like a broken gong, you reach extinguishment and know no conflict.
10:135 As a cowherd drives the cows to pasture with the rod, so too old age and death drive life from living beings.
10:136 The fool does not understand the evil that they do. But because of those deeds, that simpleton is tormented as if burnt by fire.
10:137 One who violently attacks the peaceful and the innocent swiftly falls to one of ten bad states:
10:138 harsh pain; loss; the breakup of the body; serious illness; mental distress;
10:139 hazards from rulers; vicious slander; loss of kin; destruction of wealth;
10:140 or else their home is consumed by fire. When their body breaks up, that witless person is reborn in hell.
10:141 Not nudity, nor matted hair, nor mud, nor fasting, nor lying on bare ground, nor wearing dust and dirt, or squatting on the heels, will cleanse a mortal not free of doubt.
10:142 Dressed up they may be, but if they live well— peaceful, tamed, committed to the spiritual path, having laid aside violence toward all creatures— they are a brahmin, an ascetic, a mendicant.
10:143 Can a person constrained by conscience be found in the world? Who shies away from blame, like a fine horse from the whip?
10:144 Like a fine horse under the whip, be keen and full of urgency. With faith, ethics, and energy, immersion, and investigation of principles, accomplished in knowledge and conduct, mindful, give up this vast suffering.
10:145 While irrigators guide water, fletchers bend arrows straight, and carpenters bend timber straight, those true to their vows tame themselves.

Chapter 11. Old Age

11:146 What is joy, what is laughter, when the flames are ever burning? Swathed in darkness, would you not seek a light?
11:147 See this fancy puppet, a body built of sores, diseased, obsessed over, in which nothing lasts at all.
11:148 This body is decrepit and frail, a nest of disease. This foul carcass falls apart, for life ends in death.
11:149 These dove-grey bones are tossed away like dried gourds in the autumn— what joy is there in such a sight?
11:150 In this city built of bones, plastered with flesh and blood, old age and death are stashed away, along with conceit and contempt.
11:151 Fancy chariots of kings wear out, and even this body gets old. But the truth of the good never gets old— so the good proclaim to the good.
11:152 A person of little learning ages like an ox— their flesh grows, but not their wisdom.
11:153 Transmigrating through countless rebirths, I've journeyed without reward, searching for the house-builder; painful is birth again and again.
11:154 I've seen you, house-builder! You won't build a house again! Your rafters are all broken, your roof-peak demolished. The mind, set on demolition, has reached the end of cravings.
11:155 When young they spurned the spiritual path and failed to earn any wealth. Now they brood like old cranes in a pond bereft of fish.
11:156 When young they spurned the spiritual path and failed to earn any wealth. Now they lie like spent arrows, bemoaning over things past.

Chapter 12. The Self

12:157 If you knew your self as beloved, you'd look after it so well. In one of the night's three watches, an astute person would remain alert.
12:158 The astute would avoid being corrupted by first grounding themselves in what is suitable, and then instructing others.
12:159 If one were to treat oneself as one instructs another, the well-tamed indeed would tame: for the self, it seems, is hard to tame.
12:160 One is indeed the lord of oneself, for who else would be one's lord? By means of a well-tamed self, one gains a lord that's rare indeed.
12:161 For the evil that is done by oneself, born and produced in oneself, grinds down a simpleton, as diamond grinds a lesser gem.
12:162 One choked by immorality, as a sal tree by a creeper, does to themselves what a foe only wishes.
12:163 It's easy to do bad things harmful to oneself, but good things that are helpful are the hardest things to do.
12:164 On account of wicked views— scorning the guidance of the perfected ones, the noble ones living righteously— the idiot begets their own self's demise, like the bamboo bearing fruit.
12:165 For it is by oneself that evil's done, one is corrupted by oneself. It's by oneself that evil's not done, one is purified by oneself. Purity and impurity are personal matters, no one can purify another.
12:166 Never neglect what is good for yourself for the sake of another, however great. Knowing well what is good for yourself, be intent upon your heart's goal.

Chapter 13. The World

13:167 Don't resort to lowly things, don't abide in negligence, don't resort to wrong views, don't perpetuate the world.
13:168 Get up, don't be heedless, live by principle, with good conduct. For one of good conduct sleeps at ease, in this world and the next.
13:169 Live by principle, with good conduct, don't conduct yourself badly. For one of good conduct sleeps at ease, in this world and the next.
13:170 Look upon the world as a bubble or a mirage, then the King of Death won't see you.
13:171 Come, see this world decked out like a fancy royal chariot. Here fools founder, but the discerning are not chained.
13:172 He who once was heedless, but turned to heedfulness, shines upon this world like the moon freed from clouds.
13:173 Someone whose bad deed is supplanted by the good, shines upon this world, like the moon freed from clouds.
13:174 Blind is the world, few are those who clearly see. Only a handful go to heaven, like a bird freed from a net.
13:175 Swans fly by the sun's path, psychic sages fly through space. The attentive leave the world, having vanquished Māra with his legions.
13:176 When a personage, spurning the hereafter, transgresses in just one thing— lying— there is no evil they would not do.
13:177 The miserly don't ascend to heaven, it takes a fool to not praise giving. The attentive celebrate giving, and so find happiness in the hereafter.
13:178 The fruit of stream-entry is better than being the one king of the earth, than going to heaven, than lordship over all the world.

Chapter 14. The Buddhas

14:179 He whose victory may not be undone, a victory unrivaled in all the world; by what track would you trace that Buddha, who leaves no track in his infinite range?
14:180 Of craving, the weaver, the clinger, he has none: so where can he be traced? By what track would you trace that Buddha, who leaves no track in his infinite range?
14:181 The attentive intent on absorption, who love the peace of renunciation, the Buddhas, ever mindful, are envied by even the gods.
14:182 It's hard to gain a human birth; the life of mortals is hard; it's hard to hear the true teaching; the arising of Buddhas is hard.
14:183 Not to do any evil; to embrace the good; to purify one's mind: this is the instruction of the Buddhas.
14:184 Patient acceptance is the ultimate fervor. Extinguishment is the ultimate, say the Buddhas. No true renunciate injures another, nor does an ascetic hurt another.
14:185 Not speaking ill nor doing harm; restraint in the monastic code; moderation in eating; staying in remote lodgings; commitment to the higher mind— this is the instruction of the Buddhas.
14:186 Even if it were raining money, you'd not be sated in sensual pleasures. An astute person understands that sensual pleasures offer little gratification and much suffering.
14:187 Thus they find no delight even in celestial pleasures. A disciple of the fully awakened Buddha delights in the ending of craving.
14:188 So many go for refuge to mountains and forest groves, to shrines in tended parks; those people are driven by fear.
14:189 But such refuge is no sanctuary, it is no supreme refuge. By going to that refuge, you're not released from all suffering.
14:190 One gone for refuge to the Buddha, to his teaching and to the Saṅgha, sees the four noble truths with right understanding:
14:191 suffering, suffering's origin, suffering's transcendence, and the noble eightfold path that leads to the stilling of suffering.
14:192 Such refuge is a sanctuary, it is the supreme refuge. By going to that refuge, you're released from all suffering.
14:193 It's hard to find a thoroughbred man: they're not born just anywhere. A family where that attentive one is born prospers in happiness.
14:194 Happy, the arising of Buddhas! Happy, the teaching of Dhamma! Happy is the harmony of the Saṅgha, and the striving of the harmonious is happy.
14:195 When a person venerates the worthy— the Buddha or his disciple, who have transcended proliferation, and have left behind grief and lamentation,
14:196 quenched, fearing nothing from any quarter— the merit of one venerating such as these, cannot be calculated by anyone, saying it is just this much.

Chapter 15. Happiness

15:197 Let us live so very happily, loving among the hostile. Among hostile humans, let us live with love.
15:198 Let us live so very happily, healthy among the ailing. Among ailing humans let us live healthily.
15:199 Let us live so very happily, content among the greedy. Among greedy humans, let us live content.
15:200 Let us live so very happily, we who have nothing. We shall feed on rapture, like the gods of sublime luminosity.
15:201 Victory breeds enmity; the defeated sleep badly. The peaceful sleep at ease, having left victory and defeat behind.
15:202 There is no fire like greed, no crime like hate, no suffering like the aggregates, no bliss beyond peace.
15:203 Hunger is the worst illness, conditions are the worst suffering. When one has known this as it is, extinguishment is the ultimate happiness.
15:204 Health is the ultimate blessing; contentment, the ultimate wealth; trust is the ultimate family; extinguishment, the ultimate happiness.
15:205 Having drunk the nectar of seclusion and the nectar of peace— free of stress, free of evil, drink the joyous nectar of truth.
15:206 It's good to see the noble ones, staying with them is always good. Were you not to see fools, you'd always be happy.
15:207 For one who consorts with fools grieves long. Painful is living with fools, like being stuck with your enemy. Happy is living with an attentive one, like meeting with your kin.
15:208 Therefore: An attentive one, wise and learned, a behemoth of virtue, true to their vows, noble: follow a true and intelligent person such as this, as the moon tracks the path of the stars.

Chapter 16. The Beloved

16:209 Applying yourself where you ought not, neglecting what you should be doing, forgetting your goal, you cling to what you hold dear, jealous of those devoted to their heart's goal.
16:210 Don't ever get too close to those you like or dislike. For not seeing the liked is suffering, and so is seeing the disliked.
16:211 Therefore don't hold anything dear, for it's bad to lose those you love. No ties are found in they who hold nothing loved or loathed.
16:212 Sorrow springs from what we hold dear, fear springs from what we hold dear; one free from holding anything dear has no sorrow, let alone fear.
16:213 Sorrow springs from attachment, fear springs from attachment; one free from attachment has no sorrow, let alone fear.
16:214 Sorrow springs from relishing, fear springs from relishing; one free from relishing has no sorrow, let alone fear.
16:215 Sorrow springs from desire, fear springs from desire; one free from desire has no sorrow, let alone fear.
16:216 Sorrow springs from craving, fear springs from craving; one free from craving has no sorrow, let alone fear.
16:217 One accomplished in virtue and vision, firmly principled, and truthful, doing oneself what ought be done: that's who the people love.
16:218 One eager to realize the ineffable would be filled with awareness. Their mind not bound to pleasures of sense, they're said to be heading upstream.
16:219 When a man returns safely after a long time spent abroad, family, friends, and loved ones celebrate his return.
16:220 Just so, when one who has done good goes from this world to the next, their good deeds receive them there, as family welcomes home one they love.

Chapter 17. Anger

17:221 Give up anger, get rid of conceit, and escape every fetter. Sufferings don't befall one who has nothing, not clinging to name and form.
17:222 When anger surges like a lurching chariot, keep it in check. That's what I call a charioteer; others just hold the reins.
17:223 Defeat anger with kindness, villainy with virtue, stinginess with giving, and lies with truth.
17:224 Speak the truth, do not be angry, and give when asked, if only a little. By these three means, you may enter the presence of the gods.
